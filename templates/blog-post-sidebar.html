Selectivity, indexes, and why your report times out at fifty thousand rows while the same query is instant in a sandbox with four hundred.

A query that returns in ninety milliseconds against four hundred records and times out against four hundred thousand has not got slower. It has stopped being selective, and the optimiser has quietly changed its mind about how to run it.

What selectivity means to the optimiser

Selectivity is not a property of a field. It is a property of a filter against the data actually in the table right now: the proportion of rows a WHERE clause eliminates. A status field with two values is unselective on the common value and highly selective on the rare one, and the same query is fast for one user and slow for another for exactly that reason.

TipIf a query is fast in a sandbox and slow in production, do not look at the query. Look at how many rows the filter removes in each.

Which fields are actually indexed

Standard indexes exist on the id, the name, the owner, the audit fields, and any field marked unique or as an external id. Everything else is a custom index somebody asked for — and a custom index on a field that is never selective buys nothing.

The thresholds, and where they come from

A standard index is considered when the filter matches under 30% of the first million rows and under 15% of everything above that, capped at a million matches. A custom index is tighter: 10%, and 333,333.

Watch outThese are thresholds, not promises. The optimiser can decline an index it is entitled to use, and the query plan is the only thing that tells you it did.

Reading a query plan without guessing

The developer console’s query plan tool gives every candidate plan a cost. Under 1.0 means an index will be used; at or above it, a full table scan. That number is the entire conversation.
