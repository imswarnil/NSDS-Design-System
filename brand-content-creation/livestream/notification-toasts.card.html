Notification toasts — the six events

A toast is an event, not an ask: something a viewer just did, acknowledged on screen so the room can see itself. One lane, bottom-right above the watermark, one toast at a time — they queue rather than stack. Enters from the right over 240ms, holds, leaves the same way. If more arrive than the lane can clear, drop them; a backlog of thank-yous is worse than none.

Where they go, and for how long

CHIP LANEBottom-left, above the handle bugLeft, because the bottom-right corner is the camera and the mark. Chips sit inside the 90% title-safe box and wrap to a second row never — two is the ceiling.
STINGBottom-centre, 6sCentre is deliberate: it is the only graphic that interrupts, so it does not pretend to be furniture. Twice an hour, after a completed demo.
TOAST LANEBottom-right, above the watermark, 1 at a timeSame corner as the camera in most scenes — so when the camera is on, the toast lane moves up above it rather than overlapping. It never covers the mark.
ENTER / EXITSlide from the nearest edge, 240ms, --ease-outThe same motion the product uses for a drawer. No bounce, no scale, no sound. Under prefers-reduced-motion in a browser source, they cut.
QUIET SCENESCode focus, Q&A and guest answers suppress everythingBind a single “mute overlays” hotkey rather than trusting yourself to remember. A toast over somebody else's answer is rude in a way the viewer will read as yours.

Do

  • One ask on screen at a time. The filled chip is the ask; everything else is a reminder.
  • Give every overlay an exit rule and honour it. An overlay with no timer becomes furniture, and furniture is what makes a frame look busy.
  • Say the name. A toast the host never acknowledges teaches the room that the graphics are automated and nobody is watching.

Never

  • A row of five social icons. One destination at a time, rotating; a viewer who has to choose goes nowhere.
  • Stacked toasts. They queue. Three alerts sliding over each other is a notification centre, not a broadcast.
  • Animated, looping or pulsing chips. Motion that repeats cannot be dismissed and cannot be ignored — it is the fastest way to make a stream unwatchable.

Do

  • Use the edge colour as a category, not decoration. Brand for a free action, amber for paid support, green for a milestone, red for a problem.
  • Show a paid message verbatim or not at all — never paraphrased, never trimmed to fit.
  • Clear a technical notice by hand the second the problem is fixed. It is the only toast with no timer, because the problem does not have one either.

Never

  • A toast during a demo's critical moment. Suppress the lane; the queue will still be there afterwards.
  • Vanity milestones. Concurrent viewers mean something to the room; total channel subscribers mean something only to you.
  • A toast on top of the watermark or the camera. The lane moves; the mark never does.
